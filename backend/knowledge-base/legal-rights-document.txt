I. Federal / Constitutional Rights (Full Universe)
________________________________________
A. U.S. Constitution (Original Articles)
Article I – Limits on Government Power
Writ of Habeas Corpus (Art. I, §9)
Content:
 The government may not detain a person indefinitely without allowing them to challenge the legality of their detention before a court, except in cases of rebellion or invasion.
Example:
 If someone is arrested, they must eventually be brought before a judge. The government cannot “disappear” someone or hold them forever without charges.
________________________________________
Prohibition on Bills of Attainder
Content:
 Congress may not pass laws that declare a person or group guilty and punish them without a trial.
Example:
 Congress cannot pass a law stating “Person X is guilty of a crime and shall be imprisoned.” Guilt must be determined by courts, not lawmakers.
________________________________________
Prohibition on Ex Post Facto Laws
Content:
 The government cannot criminalize behavior retroactively or increase punishment after the fact.
Example:
 If an action was legal in 2022, the government cannot later make it illegal and punish someone for doing it in 2022.
________________________________________
Article III – Judicial Rights
Right to Trial by Jury for Federal Crimes
Content:
 Federal criminal trials must be decided by a jury, not solely by judges or government officials.
Example:
 If someone is charged with a federal crime, the government cannot convict them through an administrative process alone.
________________________________________
Crimes Defined by Law, Not Executive Action
Content:
 Only Congress can define crimes; the executive branch cannot invent crimes by decree.
Example:
 The President or a federal agency cannot decide that a new action is suddenly criminal unless Congress has passed a law.

Rights Around Warrants
________________________________________
1. Warrant Requirement (Fourth Amendment)
Legal text:
“The right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects, against unreasonable searches and seizures, shall not be violated, and no Warrants shall issue, but upon probable cause, supported by Oath or affirmation, and particularly describing the place to be searched, and the persons or things to be seized.”
 — U.S. Constitution, Fourth Amendment
Example:
 Police generally need a warrant to enter a home, search a phone, or seize personal property.
________________________________________
2. Probable Cause Requirement
Legal rule:
 A warrant must be supported by probable cause, meaning specific facts indicating evidence of a crime will be found.
Authority:
 Illinois v. Gates (1983)
Holding (rule):
Probable cause is determined by a “totality of the circumstances.”
Example:
 An officer’s hunch or anonymous tip alone is not enough for a warrant.
________________________________________
3. Particularity Requirement
Legal rule:
 A warrant must specifically identify:
●	the place to be searched

●	the items or persons to be seized

Authority:
 Maryland v. Garrison (1987)
Example:
 A warrant for “Apartment 3B” does not allow police to search the entire building.
________________________________________
4. Judicial Approval Requirement
Legal rule:
 A warrant must be issued by a neutral and detached magistrate, not police.
Authority:
 Johnson v. United States (1948)
Holding:
The Fourth Amendment requires that inferences be drawn by “a neutral and detached magistrate instead of being judged by the officer engaged in the often competitive enterprise of ferreting out crime.”
Example:
 Police cannot approve their own warrants.
________________________________________
5. Right to See the Warrant
Legal rule:
 Occupants may ask to see the warrant and verify:
●	address

●	scope

●	judge’s signature

Authority:
 Groh v. Ramirez (2004)
Holding:
A warrant that fails to describe the items to be seized is invalid on its face.
Example:
 If officers refuse to show a warrant, entry may be unlawful.
________________________________________
6. Limits on Scope of Search
Legal rule:
 Police may search only what the warrant authorizes.
Authority:
 Marron v. United States (1927)
Holding:
“The requirement that warrants shall particularly describe the things to be seized makes general searches impossible.”
Example:
 A warrant for a firearm does not allow police to read personal letters.
________________________________________
7. Time Limits on Warrants
Legal rule:
 Warrants must be executed within a reasonable time (often specified by statute).
Authority:
 Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure, Rule 41
Example:
 A warrant issued weeks ago may be invalid if not timely executed.
________________________________________
8. Knock-and-Announce Rule
Legal rule:
 Police must generally:
●	knock

●	announce presence and purpose

●	wait a reasonable time

Authority:
 Wilson v. Arkansas (1995)
Holding:
The common-law knock-and-announce principle is part of the Fourth Amendment reasonableness inquiry.
Example:
 Police cannot normally break down a door without warning.
________________________________________
9. No-Knock Warrants (Exception)
Legal rule:
 No-knock entry allowed only if officers reasonably believe announcing would:
●	endanger safety

●	allow destruction of evidence

●	be futile

Authority:
 Richards v. Wisconsin (1997)
Example:
 No-knock warrants are not automatic and require justification.
________________________________________
10. Exigent Circumstances (Warrant Exception)
Legal rule:
 Police may enter without a warrant only in emergencies.
Recognized exigencies include:
●	imminent danger

●	hot pursuit

●	destruction of evidence

Authority:
 Kentucky v. King (2011)
Example:
 Police may enter a home to stop an ongoing assault.
________________________________________
11. Consent Is Not a Warrant
Legal rule:
 Consent must be voluntary and can be refused.
Authority:
 Schneckloth v. Bustamonte (1973)
Holding:
Consent must be “freely and voluntarily given.”
Example:
 Saying “I do not consent to any searches” preserves rights even if a search proceeds.
________________________________________
12. Warrants for Arrest vs Search
Arrest Warrant
●	Authorizes arrest of a person

●	Does not automatically authorize search of a home

Authority:
 Payton v. New York (1980)
Example:
 Police need a search warrant (or exigent circumstances) to enter a third party’s home.
________________________________________
Search Warrant
●	Authorizes search of a specific location

●	Does not authorize arrest unless specified

________________________________________
13. Invalid Warrants
Legal rule:
 Evidence obtained via an invalid warrant may be excluded.
Authority:
 Mapp v. Ohio (1961)
Holding:
Evidence obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment is inadmissible in state courts.
Example:
 An unsigned or overly broad warrant can invalidate a search.
________________________________________
14. Digital Warrants & Privacy
Legal rule:
 Digital data receives heightened protection.
Authority:
 Riley v. California (2014)
Holding:
Police generally must obtain a warrant to search a cellphone.
Example:
 Police cannot search phone contents during arrest without a warrant.
________________________________________
15. Warrants Apply to All Persons
Legal rule:
 Fourth Amendment protections apply to all persons, not just citizens.
Authority:
 United States v. Verdugo-Urquidez (1990) (domestic protections)
Example:
 Non-citizens in the U.S. have warrant protections for homes and property.

















General Laws on Leasing, Homeownership, and Property Rights
I. Foundational Property Rights
Right to Exclude
Rule: Owners and lawful occupants have the right to exclude others from their property.
 Authority: Common law property doctrine; recognized nationwide.
Example:
 A landlord or homeowner can deny entry to non-emergency visitors.
________________________________________
Right to Quiet Enjoyment
Rule: Tenants are entitled to use their dwelling without unreasonable interference.
 Authority: Implied covenant of quiet enjoyment (common law; adopted in all states).
Example:
 A landlord cannot repeatedly enter a unit without notice or harass a tenant.
________________________________________
Protection Against Unreasonable Searches
Rule: Homes receive the highest Fourth Amendment protection.
 Authority: U.S. Constitution, Fourth Amendment.
Example:
 Police generally need a warrant to enter a home, regardless of ownership or tenancy.
________________________________________
II. Leasing (Tenant Rights & Duties)
Lease as a Contract
Rule: A lease is a binding contract defining rights and obligations of landlord and tenant.
 Authority: State contract law (uniformly recognized).
Example:
 Rent amount, term, and rules must follow the written lease.
________________________________________
Habitability
Rule: Landlords must provide a habitable dwelling.
 Authority: Implied Warranty of Habitability (common law; codified in many states).
Minimum standards typically include:
●	Heat, water, electricity

●	Weatherproofing

●	Basic safety

Example:
 A landlord must fix broken heat in winter.
________________________________________
Landlord Entry
Rule: Landlords may enter only with proper notice, except emergencies.
 Authority: Common law; codified in most state landlord-tenant statutes.
Example:
 A landlord must give advance notice before repairs unless there is a fire or flood.
________________________________________
Eviction Protections
Rule: Tenants cannot be removed without due process.
 Authority: Due Process Clause (14th Amendment) + state eviction laws.
Example:
 Lockouts without court orders are illegal.
________________________________________
Security Deposits
Rule: Deposits must be handled according to law and returned subject to lawful deductions.
 Authority: State statutes (generally uniform in principle).
Example:
 A landlord cannot withhold a deposit without reason.
________________________________________
III. Homeownership Rights
Right to Possess and Use Property
Rule: Owners may occupy, rent, or use property subject to zoning and law.
 Authority: Common law property rights.
Example:
 An owner may live in or lease their home.
________________________________________
Protection from Government Taking
Rule: Property may not be taken for public use without just compensation.
 Authority: U.S. Constitution, Fifth Amendment (Takings Clause).
Example:
 If land is taken for a highway, the owner must be paid fair value.
________________________________________
Due Process in Foreclosure
Rule: Homeowners are entitled to notice and legal process before foreclosure.
 Authority: Due Process Clause (14th Amendment); state foreclosure laws.
Example:
 Banks cannot seize a home without court procedures or statutory compliance.
________________________________________
IV. Property & Law Enforcement
Consent to Entry
Rule: Police may enter only with a warrant, consent, or exigent circumstances.
 Authority: Payton v. New York (1980).
Example:
 Saying “I do not consent to entry” preserves rights.
________________________________________
Tenant vs Owner Consent
Rule: Lawful occupants (tenants) can consent to entry into their own dwelling.
 Authority: United States v. Matlock (1974).
Example:
 A landlord generally cannot consent to police entry into a tenant’s unit.
________________________________________
Common Areas
Rule: Common areas may have reduced privacy expectations.
 Authority: Katz v. United States (reasonable expectation of privacy).
Example:
 Hallways in apartment buildings often receive less protection than units.
________________________________________
V. Property Disputes & Remedies
Right to Sue
Rule: Owners and tenants may bring civil actions for violations.
 Authority: State civil procedure laws; federal civil rights statutes where applicable.
Example:
 Tenants can sue for unlawful eviction; owners can sue for trespass.
________________________________________
Non-Discrimination
Rule: Housing discrimination is prohibited.
 Authority: Fair Housing Act (Civil Rights Act of 1968).
Protected classes include:
●	Race, color, religion

●	Sex (including sexual orientation & gender identity)

●	National origin

●	Disability

●	Familial status

Example:
 A landlord cannot refuse to rent based on race or disability.
________________________________________
VI. Plain-Language Summary (Presentation-Ready)
U.S. property law protects the right to occupy, exclude others, and enjoy property without unreasonable interference; tenants receive due process, habitability, and privacy protections, while owners receive possession, compensation, and foreclosure safeguards, all subject to constitutional limits on government action.


















Traffic stops, police questioning, and federal immigration enforcement (ICE)
1. Traffic Stops – Police
Right: Police Need Reasonable Suspicion to Stop You
Principle: Police cannot stop your vehicle arbitrarily — they must have specific, articulable facts suggesting a law violation or criminal activity.
 Source: Federal Fourth Amendment interpretation via reasonable suspicion doctrine (e.g., as summarized by legal analysis).
Example:
 A traffic stop for weaving, excessive speed, or a broken taillight can be lawful; stopping vehicles based solely on out-of-state plates without other suspicion was ruled unconstitutional by a federal appeals court.
________________________________________
Right: You Have Constitutional Protections During a Traffic Stop
Principle: Stops are considered “seizures” under the Fourth Amendment, and you retain rights against unreasonable search and seizure.
 Source: ACLU/Southern California “Know Your Rights” guide.
Examples:
●	You must provide your driver’s license, registration, and proof of insurance when asked.

●	You have the right to remain silent beyond identifying yourself and your documents.

●	You can refuse consent to a vehicle search; police need probable cause or a warrant to search without consent.

________________________________________
2. Police Questioning
Right: Right to Remain Silent
Principle: You generally do not have to answer questions beyond basic identification during a stop or encounter — even if the officer persists.
 Source: ACLU/ACLU SoCal “Know Your Rights.”
Example:
 When police ask you about your destination or activities, you can say “I choose to remain silent.”
________________________________________
Right: Right to Refuse Searches (Without Probable Cause)
Principle: You can refuse to consent to a search of your person, vehicle, or belongings; consent must be voluntary.
 Sources: ACLU SoCal; general constitutional protections.
Example:
 If an officer says “Can I look in your trunk?” you may respond “I do not consent to this search.”
________________________________________
Right: Right to Ask if You Are Free to Leave
Principle: If you are not being detained or arrested, you can ask whether you are free to go.
 Source: ACLU SoCal rights guide.
Example:
 Asking “Am I free to leave?” clarifies whether the stop is over or escalating into detention.
________________________________________
3. Immigration Enforcement – ICE (Federal)
Right: Constitutional Protections Apply to Everyone
Principle: Constitutional rights (e.g., Fourth, Fifth Amendment protections) apply to all persons in the U.S., regardless of immigration or citizenship status.
 Source: Immigrant Justice Project and multiple civil rights guides.
Example:
 Even if approached by ICE or immigration agents, you retain the right to remain silent and protections against unreasonable searches.
________________________________________
Right: Right to Remain Silent During ICE Questioning
Principle: You are not required to answer questions about your immigration status, place of birth, or how you entered the country.
 Sources: ACLU San Diego/Imperial; Stop AAPI Hate guide.
Example:
 ICE may ask “Where were you born?” or “Are you a citizen?” — you can say “I choose to remain silent.”
________________________________________
Right: Right to Refuse Consent to Searches by ICE
Principle: ICE and other federal agents need consent or probable cause to search you or your belongings.
 Sources: ACLU San Diego/Imperial; congressional code.
Example:
 ICE may request to search a bag or phone; you can respond “I do not consent to this search.”
________________________________________
Right: ICE Cannot Enter a Home Without a Judicial Warrant
Principle: Federal immigration agents generally cannot enter a private residence without a valid judicial warrant or an applicable exception.
 Sources: ACLU San Diego/Imperial; RI ACLU ICE KYR.
Example:
 You can not open your door to ICE without seeing a judge-signed warrant that names you and your address.
________________________________________
Right: You Can Ask ICE If You Are Free to Leave
Principle: Similar to police stops, you may ask whether you are under arrest or free to go if contacted by ICE.
 Source: GWU International Services “Know Your Rights” guide.
Example:
 Asking “Am I under arrest or free to leave?” clarifies whether the encounter is consensual or escalating.
________________________________________
ICE Statutory Authority and Limits
Statute: 8 U.S.C. § 1357 grants immigration officers power without a warrant to:
●	interrogate anyone believed to be an alien about right to be in the U.S.

●	arrest someone only if they believe the person is in violation AND likely to escape before a warrant can be obtained.

Example:
 ICE can question someone if they suspect they are not lawfully present and can arrest without warrant only when there’s immediate risk of escape.

Pennsylvania – State Constitutional Rights
Pennsylvania Constitution, Article I – Declaration of Rights
Pennsylvania courts have repeatedly held that Article I provides protections that are independent of, and sometimes broader than, the U.S. Constitution.
________________________________________
§1 – Inherent Rights of Mankind (Due Process / Liberty)
Content
“All men are born equally free and independent, and have certain inherent and indefeasible rights, among which are those of enjoying and defending life and liberty, of acquiring, possessing and protecting property and reputation, and of pursuing their own happiness.”
What this means
●	The Commonwealth must respect basic liberty and property interests.

●	Government action interfering with liberty or property must be justified by law.

Examples
●	The state cannot arbitrarily deprive someone of liberty without legal process.

●	Property cannot be taken or damaged without lawful authority.

________________________________________
§7 – Freedom of Speech and Press
Content
“The free communication of thoughts and opinions is one of the invaluable rights of man, and every citizen may freely speak, write and print on any subject, being responsible for the abuse of that liberty.”
What makes this stronger than federal law
●	Pennsylvania explicitly protects free communication, not just speech.

●	Courts interpret this provision independently of the First Amendment.

Examples
●	Peacefully criticizing police or government officials is protected.

●	Filming public officials in public spaces is generally protected expression.

________________________________________
§8 – Security from Unreasonable Searches and Seizures
Content
“The people shall be secure in their persons, houses, papers and possessions from unreasonable searches and seizures, and no warrant to search any place or to seize any person or things shall issue without describing them as nearly as may be, nor without probable cause, supported by oath or affirmation.”
What makes this stronger
●	Pennsylvania courts often require stricter warrant standards than federal law.

●	Evidence obtained in violation of §8 may be excluded even when federal law would allow it.

Examples
●	Police generally need a warrant to search a home or phone.

●	Overly broad warrants can be invalid under Pennsylvania law.

________________________________________
§9 – Rights of Accused Persons
Content
“In all criminal prosecutions the accused hath a right to be heard by himself and his counsel, to demand the nature and cause of the accusation against him, to be confronted with the witnesses against him… nor can he be compelled to give evidence against himself…”
Key protections
●	Right to counsel

●	Right to remain silent

●	Right to confront witnesses

●	Right to know the charges

Examples
●	A person cannot be forced to answer police questions.

●	Accused individuals have the right to legal representation.

________________________________________
§11 – Courts Open / Due Process
Content
“All courts shall be open; and every man for an injury done him in his lands, goods, person or reputation shall have remedy by due course of law…”
What this means
●	Access to courts is a constitutional right.

●	Government actors can be sued for unlawful harm.

Examples
●	Individuals may bring civil suits for rights violations.

●	Police misconduct can be challenged in court.

________________________________________
§13 – Excessive Bail and Punishment
Content
“Excessive bail shall not be required, nor excessive fines imposed, nor cruel punishments inflicted.”
Examples
●	Bail cannot be set at punitive levels.

●	Fines must be proportional to the offense.
